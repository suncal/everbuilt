# The web industry's real business model is renting you your own website

Ask any business owner what their website cost and they'll quote the sticker price: $3,000 to a freelancer, $35,000 to an agency, "free" on a site builder. Almost nobody quotes the number that actually matters — the monthly bleed that never ends.

A $35/month builder subscription is $2,100 over five years, for a template you assembled yourself on a platform you don't own. A typical agency's $300/month "maintenance" contract is $18,000 over the same period — frequently more than the build itself. And in both cases, stop paying and the site degrades or disappears. That's not a service relationship. That's rent.

## The test that exposes it

Ask your web vendor four questions:

1. Who owns the code and content if we part ways?
2. What happens to my site the month I stop paying you?
3. What's the all-in five-year cost, including every monthly fee?
4. Can my team update content without paying you?

Squirming on any of these tells you where the real price lives. The contract that looks cheapest up front is often the most expensive thing you'll ever sign.

## Why the rent model persists — and why it no longer has to

For most of the web's history, hosting genuinely cost money: servers, databases, patches, someone on call. The monthly fee had a reason to exist. But a modern business website — even a large one with galleries, forms, and events — can be architected as static files served from global edge networks whose free tiers include unlimited bandwidth. No server. No database. Nothing to break at 2 a.m. and no bill on the 1st of the month.

We rebuilt a national nonprofit's website on exactly this architecture: 2,100+ photos, an admin panel their team uses without touching code, an events system they update from a spreadsheet. It launched with zero minutes of downtime, cost $7,500 against agency quotes of $35,000–60,000, and has cost **$0/month in hosting since launch day**. That's not a promotional rate. It's physics: nothing in the architecture accrues a bill.

## Own the deed, not the lease

When you own a website outright — the code, the content, the domain, the admin keys — the power dynamic flips. Fire your vendor and lose nothing. Hand the repository to any developer alive. Your site keeps running whether or not you ever pay anyone again.

Vendors resist this model for an obvious reason: it ends the annuity. Which is exactly why "who owns it?" is the first question worth asking — and why "you do, entirely" is the answer worth paying for once instead of forever.
